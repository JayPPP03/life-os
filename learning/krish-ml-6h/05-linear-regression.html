🤔 Why this formula — feel the intuition

🎯 The business problem: You run marketing at an Adani-style firm. Four months of history: ad spend (₹ lakh) and the revenue it generated (₹ lakh). The CFO asks: "If we spend 5 next month, what revenue should we expect — and is ad spend even working?" Linear regression is the machine that turns that history into a number you can defend in the boardroom.

🧠 Why THIS formula:

  • y = θ0 + θ1·x (the hypothesis) — measures: the simplest possible relationship, one input → one output, a straight line with only two dials. Why this form: θ1 answers "how hard does ad spend push revenue?" (every extra ₹1L moves revenue by θ1 = ₹1.9L here) and θ0 anchors where you start. What breaks with a fancier curve: with 4 months of data a wiggly curve can pass through every point perfectly and still be fiction for month 5. Business consequence: you'd set next year's ad budget on noise.
  • (y − ŷ)² (squared error) — measures: the total pain of wrong forecasts. Why square? (a) Plain errors CANCEL: a +10 miss and a −10 miss would sum to zero and read "perfect forecast" — disaster for a revenue report. (b) Big misses are punished MUCH harder: a 2× miss costs 4× — a festival stockout hurts far more than a small overstock. (c) Squaring gives a smooth parabola, so gradient descent always has a clean downhill to walk. What breaks with |error| instead: a sharp corner at zero where the gradient is undefined — the walker freezes exactly at the bottom. Business consequence: no corners → training never stalls, and outliers (the misses that actually lose money) get fought hardest.
  • 1/(2m) — measures: an average, not a sum. The ÷m makes cost comparable across dataset sizes (4 months vs 400 months, same yardstick); the ÷2 is pure calculus kindness — the derivative of (…)² emits a 2, and it cancels. Business consequence: "cost per month" comparisons across campaigns stop being fooled by bigger data "looking" costlier.
  • θ := θ − α·∂J/∂θ (gradient descent) — measures: a blindfolded downhill walk on the cost parabola: feel the slope under your feet (∂J/∂θ), step the OPPOSITE way (the minus), sized by α. Too-big α → leap over the valley, each bounce bigger (α = 0.3 exploded to J = 48,072 below); too-small α → walk forever (α = 0.01 needs 1000+ steps). Business consequence: α too big = forecasts swinging wildly week to week; α too small = you are still tuning when the quarter ends.
  • R² = 1 − SSres/SStot — measures: "how much better than guessing the average?" SStot is the error of the dumbest baseline (predict the mean revenue every month); SSres is your line's error. R² = 0 → your model is AS GOOD AS the mean line; R² = 0.963 → it explains 96.3% of the variation; NEGATIVE → WORSE than guessing the average — fire the model. Business consequence: it is the honest answer to "is this model earning its keep, or should I just use last year's average?"

⚡ Learning Rate (α) — the step size of gradient descent

The intuition: gradient descent is a blindfolded man walking downhill on the cost parabola. He can FEEL the slope under his feet (∂J/∂θ), but not see the valley. The learning rate α is his step size.

update rule: θj := θj − α · ∂J/∂θj   ← α decides HOW BIG each step is

Why α=0.3 explodes here: each step is α × slope. On the steep part of the parabola the slope is big, so α=0.3 takes a huge jump PAST the minimum — landing even higher on the other side. Next step is even bigger. Death spiral.

How to choose α in practice:

  • Try a log-scale range: 0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1 — and plot J vs iteration
  • Loss curve smooth and falling → α is fine
  • Loss spikes/jumps around → α too big — cut it 10×
  • Loss barely moves → α too small — raise it 10×
  • In deep learning, optimizers like Adam auto-adjust the step size per parameter — that's exactly why they exist

💡 Interview angle: 'How do you choose the learning rate?' → 'Log-scale search + loss-curve check: smooth descent = good, oscillation = too big, flatline = too small. In deep learning I'd let Adam handle per-parameter scaling.' Bonus point: 'the divergence boundary also depends on feature scaling — that's WHY we standardize features before gradient descent.'

✍️ Solve on Paper — Linear Regression

Close the laptop. This is the pen-and-paper version — the code only works because THIS math works.

The recipe (do these steps, in order, every time):

  1. Hypothesis: write the line you are hunting: ŷ = θ0 + θ1·x.
  2. Cost: score any candidate pair with J(θ0,θ1) = (1/2m)·Σ(ŷ−y)².
  3. Gradients: ∂J/∂θ0 = (1/m)·Σ(ŷ−y)  and  ∂J/∂θ1 = (1/m)·Σ(ŷ−y)·x.
  4. Update both: θj := θj − α·gradient.
  5. Repeat 2–4 until J stops falling; judge the final line with R² = 1 − SSres/SStot.

def J(th0, th1):

th0, th1, alpha = 0.0, 0.0, 0.1

📝 Notes

The idea: draw the best straight line through points so predictions are as close as possible to reality. Everything below is just making 'best' precise.

  • Hypothesis (the line): hθ(x) = θ0 + θ1·x. θ0 = intercept (where the line meets the y-axis), θ1 = slope/coefficient.
  • 'Best line' = the one minimizing the distances between predicted points and actual points.
Cost: J(θ0, θ1) = (1/2m) · Σᵢ (hθ(xᵢ) − yᵢ)²
  • Squared error cost: square each vertical error (kills negatives), sum, average over m points. The extra ÷2 exists purely to cancel the power-2 in the derivative later.
  • Worked example (points (1,1), (2,2), (3,3)): with θ1=1 → J=0 (perfect fit); θ1=0.5 → J≈0.58; θ1=0 → J≈2.3. Plotting J against θ1 gives a convex parabola.
  • Convexity → one global minimum: linear regression's cost surface has NO local minima. (Deep learning nets DO have local minima — that's why optimizers like Adam/RMSprop exist there.)
  • Gradient descent: walk downhill on the cost parabola: θⱼ := θⱼ − α·∂J/∂θⱼ, with derivatives ∂J/∂θ0 = (1/m)·Σ(h−y) and ∂J/∂θ1 = (1/m)·Σ(h−y)·x.
  • Learning rate α: too big → you overshoot the minimum and may diverge; too small → painfully slow convergence.
R² = 1 − SSres / SStot   ·   Adjusted R² = 1 − (1−R²)(n−1)/(n−p−1)
  • R²: share of variance explained. Can be NEGATIVE if your line is worse than just predicting the mean.
  • Adjusted R²: punishes useless features. Video example: predicting salary — adding bedrooms raised R² 85→90, adding location 90→91, adding gender 91→… but adjusted R² fell 86→82: the features added noise, not signal.
  • 💡 Interview angle: 'Which is bigger, R² or adjusted R²?' → R² is always ≥ adjusted R² (adding features can never lower raw R²; adjusted R² can fall). Also be ready to compute R² from SSres/SStot and to explain why we divide by 2m.
