🤔 Why this formula — feel the intuition

🎯 The business problem: A housing team wants an automated price estimate: rooms + age → price in ₹ lakh. Before anyone trusts the number, three boring-sounding choices decide everything: do you understand the formula you shipped, did you test it honestly, and can you decode what sklearn printed. Get any of these wrong and you quote a client a price promise that collapses on the very first new house.

🧠 Why THIS formula:

  • Why still compute θ by hand ONCE, even though sklearn exists — measures: ownership, not speed. When sklearn says price = 18.84·rooms + 0.16·age − 13.21, you know it solved exactly the closed form you did on paper — Σ(x−x̄)(y−ȳ)/Σ(x−x̄)² — so when age's sign comes out POSITIVE (older houses pricier?) you read it correctly as "5 rows, correlated features, shaky coefficients" instead of "the library is broken". Business consequence: you can defend the ₹82.9L estimate in a review with your own derivation, not with "the library said so".
  • cross_val_score with cv = 5 — measures: how the model prices houses it has NEVER seen, five times, so no single split gets to flatter it. Why 5 folds: one split can LIE — on the colony below, one fold reports MSE 3.78 ("misses of ₹1.9L, ship it!") while another reports 34.03 (a disaster); the average 12.13 is the honest verdict, because every house takes its turn as the test jury. What breaks with a single split: you can pick — or stumble into — the lucky split and fool yourself completely. Business consequence: the promise "we miss by about ₹3.5L on average" survives contact with listings the model has never seen.
  • Why sklearn reports NEG mean squared error — measures: the same MSE with the sign flipped, by sklearn's convention: it maximizes scores (bigger = better), and MSE is a loss (smaller = better), so losses come out negated — −12.13 BEATS −171.68. What breaks if you forget the convention: you would see −171.68 > −12.13, declare Ridge α = 1 the "winner", and ship a model 14× worse. Business consequence: decode the sign first, then speak: "neg MSE −12.13 → MSE 12.13 → RMSE ≈ ₹3.5L average miss." One sentence, fully defensible.

📈 See it:

💬 Feel it: Picking your best batsman off ONE flashy innings is a single split — flat pitch, lucky edges, 99 not out. Picking him on his average across five matches is cv = 5: a different jury every match, and the average tells you who actually shows up. And sklearn's negative score is just the scoreboard printing "runs conceded" — lower is better; forget that and you celebrate the worst bowler on the team.

📋 Coach's Review

Common learner gaps in this topic — check yourself against these before the quiz:

  • Panicking at the minus sign. scikit-learn maximizes scores, so loss metrics arrive negated in neg_mean_squared_error output. Flip the sign — there is no bug.
  • Thinking cv=5 builds five different models. It is the SAME estimator refit 5 times, each fold taking one turn as the test set; the 5 scores are averaged into one honest number.
  • Trusting one lucky train/test split. A single split (or a single fold's score) can fool you — quote the CV average, like the video's −37.1 mean.
  • Slicing X/y backwards. X = every column except the last (features), y = only the last (Price). Swap them and the model 'predicts features from price'.
  • Reporting a bare MSE without units. An MSE of 12.13 means nothing until translated: RMSE ≈ 3.5 → 'we miss house prices by ≈ ₹3.5 lakh on average'.
  • Calling load_boston in modern scikit-learn. It was removed over ethical concerns — knowing this (and the alternatives) signals you keep up with the libraries on your resume.

Honesty note: age's tiny positive sign makes no business sense (older houses pricier?) — with 5 rows and rooms/age moving together, individual coefficients get shaky. Judge the model by CV error, not coefficient signs.

Step 3 — cross_val_score, cv=5 (real run):

fold scores: [−34.03, −3.78, −12.41, −4.18, −6.25]  →  mean = −12.13

Why is fold 1 so terrible? With 5 rows, each fold holds out exactly one house — predicting the 3-room H1 from the other four means extrapolating below the training range. Tiny data → wild folds → this is exactly why we average 5 of them instead of trusting one split.

Step 5 — predict a new house (5 rooms, 12 years): 18.8421×5 + 0.1579×12 − 13.2105 = ₹82.89 lakh.

✍️ Solve on Paper — Least Squares by Hand

Close the laptop. Pen and paper. The code works because this math works.

The recipe:

  1. Means: x̄ = Σx/n and ȳ = Σy/n.
  2. Deviations: for every row write down (x−x̄) and (y−ȳ).
  3. Slope: θ1 = Σ(x−x̄)(y−ȳ) / Σ(x−x̄)².
  4. Intercept: θ0 = ȳ − θ1·x̄.
  5. Check: predict every training row; residual = y − ŷ.
  6. Grade the line: R² = 1 − SSres/SStot.

📝 Notes

The dataset: Boston housing
from sklearn.datasets import load_boston data = load_boston() df = pd.DataFrame(data.data, columns=data.feature_names) df['Price'] = data.target
  • 13 numeric features (rooms, crime rate, age, ...) and one target column: house Price. Every row is one observation; the goal is to predict the price from the 13 features.
Features and target
X = df.iloc[:,:-1] y = df.iloc[:,-1]
  • X takes every column except the last; y takes just the Price column.
Train + cross-validate in one move
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score scores = cross_val_score(model, X, y, scoring='neg_mean_squared_error', cv=5) np.mean(scores) ≈ −37.1
  • cv = 5 splits the data into 5 folds. The model is trained and evaluated 5 separate times, each fold taking one turn as the test set, and the 5 scores get averaged. cv = 5 → 5 fits, 5 scores, 1 average.
  • Concretely: fold 1 is the test set while folds 2–5 train; then fold 2 takes its turn, and so on until every fold has been the test set exactly once.
  • Why bother? One lucky train/test split can fool you. Averaging 5 folds makes the estimate honest — you quote one stable number instead of five swinging ones.
  • The scores come out negative because scikit-learn maximizes scores, so error metrics are handed over negated. Flip the sign: MSE ≈ 37.13.
Predict on new data
model.predict(new_data) → predicted prices
  • Fit the model, hand it new feature rows, get price predictions back.
