📋 Coach's Review

Common learner gaps in this topic — check yourself against each before the quiz:

① Off-by-one on MinPts.
The point ITSELF counts toward MinPts (sklearn's min_samples convention). Count 8 neighbors vs 7 and core flips to border — the single most common hand-tracing error.
② Counting diagonal grid neighbors.
On a unit grid the diagonal neighbor is √2 ≈ 1.41 away; with ε = 1.1 it does NOT count. Distance, not board-game adjacency, decides.
③ Calling border points noise.
A border point has few neighbors of its own but sits within ε of a CORE point — it joins that cluster. Only points near NO core are noise.
④ Expecting to choose K.
DBSCAN takes ε and MinPts, never K — the cluster count falls out of the density (below: 2 clusters + noise, K never given).
⑤ Running ε on unscaled features.
ε is a radius in feature units; if spend runs to 65,000 and visits to 27, one ε cannot serve both axes. Standardize first.
⑥ Ignoring ε extremes.
ε too large merges everything into one blob; ε too small brands everything noise. Tune the pair (ε, MinPts) together.

📝 Notes

The name is the syllabus

DBSCAN = Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise. It builds clusters out of dense regions of points instead of distances to centroids — and it ships with a built-in plan for points that belong nowhere.

ELI10: at a party, a huddle of people is a group; someone standing at the edge of the huddle still belongs to it; a lone person in the corner joins nobody — and DBSCAN is honest about that instead of forcing them into a conversation.

Two hyperparameters
  • EPSILON (ε) — the radius of the neighborhood drawn around each point.
  • MinPts — the minimum number of points required inside ε for the neighborhood to count as dense.

Tuning these two is the whole game: a larger ε lets neighborhoods absorb more ground, while a larger MinPts demands thicker regions — and pushes more sparse points into the noise bucket.

Three kinds of points
  • Core point — has at least MinPts neighbors within ε: it lives inside a dense region and anchors a cluster.
  • Border point — has fewer than MinPts neighbors of its own, but sits within ε of a core point, so it joins that cluster from the edge.
  • NOISE point — neither of the above. It is treated as an outlier and is NOT forced into any cluster.
The superpower vs K-means

K-means must put every single point into some cluster — so outliers get absorbed, and a few extreme points drag the centroids away from the real groups. DBSCAN refuses: sparse, lonely points become noise and stay out. Second superpower: because clusters grow wherever density connects them, DBSCAN finds arbitrarily shaped clusters — rings, moons, snakes. The comparison picture: K-means splits the data into convex blobs (every point just goes to its nearest center), while DBSCAN follows the density shapes. On a ring-shaped dataset, K-means cuts the ring into two half-moon blobs by nearest center; DBSCAN traces the ring itself.

💡 Interview angle: K-means vs DBSCAN on outliers? — K-means absorbs every point, so outliers corrupt the means; DBSCAN labels them noise, which is why it also doubles as an anomaly detector.
