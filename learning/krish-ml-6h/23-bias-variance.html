① Reciting "bias = underfit, variance = overfit" without the definitions.
Bias = error on the TRAINING data (can't even fit the pattern); variance = how much performance swings when the data changes (memorized noise that doesn't transfer). Two independent axes, not two names for one thing.
② "Underfit = high bias + LOW variance."
The video's frame says underfit fails BOTH: train 60 / test 55. It can't fit the train pattern AND it doesn't transfer.
③ Believing training accuracy can detect overfitting.
It can't. Below, degree 3 AND degree 9 both hit train MSE ≈ 0 — only held-out data exposes the memorizer. Exactly why your reco tuning touched test once, at the end.
④ "A more complex model is always better."
Extra capacity fits the NOISE too — the degree-9 blow-up below (test MSE 147,735) is the invoice for that.
⑤ Vague on the actual levers.

Read it against the 2×2 grid:

  • degree 1 = high bias + high variance: a straight line can't bend — test predictions 5.57 and 9.78 vs actual 2.0 and 7.8. Fails train AND test.
  • degree 3 = low bias + low variance: test predictions 2.13 and 8.12 vs actual 2.0 and 7.8 — misses of 0.13 and 0.32. Ship this one.
  • degree 9 = low bias + high variance: 10 free parameters memorize 4 points (train MSE 0.0000), then predict 551 at x = 4 where reality is 7.8. Textbook overfit.
Fix UNDERFIT (degree 1): more capacity → degree 3.    Fix OVERFIT (degree 9): regularize / simplify / more data.
Proof the overfit fix works: degree 9 + standardize + Ridge(α=0.01) → test MSE 0.11, down from 147,735.

from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression
from sklearn.preprocessing import PolynomialFeatures
from sklearn.metrics import mean_squared_error

results = {}
for d in [1, 3, 9]:
    pf = PolynomialFeatures(degree=d)
    Xtr = pf.fit_transform(x[train].reshape(-1, 1))
    Xte = pf.transform(x[test].reshape(-1, 1))
    m = LinearRegression().fit(Xtr, y[train])
    results[d] = (mean_squared_error(y[train], m.predict(Xtr)),
                  mean_squared_error(y[test],  m.predict(Xte)),
                  np.round(m.predict(Xte), 2))
for d, (tr, te, preds) in results.items():
    print(f"degree {d}: train MSE = {tr:.4f}   test MSE = {te:.2f}   test preds {preds}")

from sklearn.linear_model import Ridge
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

📝 Notes

The single most-asked concept question in ML interviews, defined the video's way:

  • Bias = error from how well the model fits the TRAINING data. Fits train well → low bias; fails on train → high bias.
  • Variance = how much the model's performance CHANGES when the data changes. Performs well on TEST/new data → low variance; collapses on test → high variance.
UNDERFIT = high bias + high variance (train 60 / test 55) OVERFIT = low bias + high variance (train 90 / test 75) GENERALIZED = low bias + low variance (train 90 / test 92)
  • Model-behavior examples from the video: a model scoring train 90 / test 75 memorized the training data → overfit. Train 60 / test 55 is too simple → underfit. Train 90 / test 92 is the target — generalized.
  • How this drives decisions: overfit → simplify the model, regularize (Ridge/Lasso, λ), get more data, prune; underfit → richer model/features, train longer.
  • Deep-learning footnote: neural-network cost surfaces DO contain local minima — that's why optimizers like Adam and RMSprop exist there; linear regression's convex parabola has a single global minimum.
  • 💡 Interview angle: be ready to classify a model from two scores (train/test) in one breath, and to say WHICH lever you'd pull: 'train 95/test 70 → overfit → I'd regularize and validate properly, exactly like my recommender's validation-only tuning.'

🎯 Prove it with your projects

  • Your reco engine is an anti-overfit machine by design: hyperparameters (SVD reg_all, α) tuned on VALIDATION only, test touched once — you actively engineered the low-bias/low-variance zone.
  • The regularization you used (reg_all = {0.05, 0.1} in the SVD) is literally the bias-variance trade-off knob: more regularization = more bias, less variance.
